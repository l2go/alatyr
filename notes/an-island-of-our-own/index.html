Reviving L2JFree · Part 3

An island of our own

Version 3.2 deleted 17,129 files of a game world the project had not written and shipped a small one that it had: a village, a meadow, and a Go tool that checks every word of it.

16 October 2026 · 9 min read

Three days after 3.1.1, the server stopped shipping a world it had not written. Version 3.2.0, released on 15 October 2026, removed 17,129 files from the repository, the image, and the release archive: 15,868 HTML pages, 511 XML files, 449 Python scripts, 176 Java scripts, and the tables around them, 679,692 lines in all. In their place the image carries 28 files. They describe one island.

The problem

A game server is two things. The engine is code: it speaks the protocol, moves characters, resolves a hit. The world is data: who stands where, what they sell, what they say, what a wolf drops. In L2J the world came as a datapack, a directory that grew next to the engine for nine years.

The engine had been modernized release by release. The datapack had only been carried along, and it had three problems.

  • It was not ours. It was the one part of the project that we had not written and could not vouch for, and every image shipped all of it.
  • It was tangled with the engine. Its Java scripts were compiled by this repository's build; its Python scripts ran inside the server. The engine, in turn, took the data for granted. It expected castles, a dimensional rift, and particular doors to exist, and it failed in ways nobody saw because they always did exist.
  • It could not change. A name lived in a CSV column, again in an XML attribute, again in the body of a dialog page. Renaming something meant finding every copy. Translating it meant a second datapack.

And for all its size, it was barely tested where it mattered. Every merge started the image and took 24 clients through a session: log in, create a character, enter the world, log out. None of them ever killed anything.

The decision

A world is a directory. It holds world.properties, a catalog/ of tables loaded into the database at start, and data/ for the files the engine reads as they are. The engine is told one path and reads nothing else. The first line it logs about the world says whose it is:

World id=buyan-okolitsa version=3.2.0 license=CC-BY-SA-4.0 expected scripts=0

Before the engine could be given a new world, it had to survive having none. The empty-world job in CI starts the image on a directory with nothing in it, and the server now reaches readiness and says, one line per part, what is missing:

World has no skills
World has no armor
World has no weapons
World has no doors
World has no zones
World has no scripts

There are 34 such lines on an empty start. Each one used to be an assumption. A merchant failed in a world without castles, entering the world failed without a dimensional rift, and an hourly task looked for a door that was not there. Each became a defect of its own and was fixed as one (#242, #251, #253, #259, #257). A world that is missing entirely, as a directory, still stops the start, and the log names the configuration key and the path.

An island

In Pushkin's Tale of Tsar Saltan, ships sail past the island of Buyan on their way to Saltan's kingdom. On the island a squirrel cracks golden nuts with emerald kernels, and the shells are cast into coin.

The new world is a corner of that island. Okolitsa is a village, the word for the edge of a settlement by its gate. Past the east gate lies Lugovina, meadow ground. The coin is the Veksha, an old word for a squirrel, and the merchant explains where it comes from: "Our island coins are cast from golden nutshells."

It is enough for a beginning: arrive at the hearth, buy a drink, clear the foragers out of the cabbage, leave the wolves for later.

What the client still decides. Players use the client they already have, and the client draws the land, the faces, and the creatures. The world decides who stands where, what they sell, what they say, and what they drop. The client also shows its own names for items and skills, so the names a player reads from the server today are those of people, their titles, and their dialogs.

Every word through a key

No text of the world is written where it is used. A dialog page refers to keys:

<font color="LEVEL">{{npc.hearth-merchant.name}}</font><br>
{{dialog.hearth-merchant.greeting}}<br>
{{dialog.hearth-merchant.prices}}<br>

The text lives in one string table, world/i18n/en.tsv, 31 entries in all. Each entry has a note for whoever translates it next, with what the thing is and how long the text may be. The key names a role, not a person, so a renamed merchant does not make his key lie.

Some sentences contain numbers, and numbers are where texts go stale. The merchant's line about the potion takes its price from the shop table and the amount it heals from the skill's definition:

dialog.hearth-merchant.prices   {potion} costs {payment} and restores {heal, number, integer} HP.
payment ← price of shop 731, position 0     catalog/merchant_buylist.csv
heal    ← skill 2031, power                 data/stats/skills/seed.xml

The player reads "Zveroboy Draught costs 12 Veksha coins and restores 72 HP." Change the price in the shop and the sentence changes with it. A dialog cannot quote a price the shop does not charge.

A small tool in Go, tools/world, does the joining. world render turns the sources and the string table into the plain files the engine reads. world check fails unless the files in the repository equal a fresh render, every key is defined and used, and every text is printable ASCII. The rendered files are committed, so the server needs no Go at run time and a reviewer sees in the diff exactly what players will see. The tool is 2,131 lines; its tests run it against 44 small worlds, most of them broken in exactly one way: an unknown key, a key nobody uses, a stray non-ASCII byte, a binding that matches two rows.

For now there is one language, English, and one alphabet. The names are Slavic, written in ASCII by one transliteration table. Renaming a person is one row. A translation, when it comes, is one file.

A test that hunts

A new world needed a test that plays it. The smoke client gained a scenario in which each session:

  1. creates a character and checks that it wears its four starting items and carries no coins;
  2. leaves the village and claims a forager in the meadow;
  3. fights until it holds 12 coins, picking up what falls;
  4. buys one Zveroboy Draught and checks that exactly 12 coins left and one bottle arrived;
  5. drinks it and checks that exactly one bottle was used;
  6. fights until level 2 and learns a skill from the tutor, checking that the skill appeared and its SP were spent;
  7. logs out, logs in, and compares level, experience, SP, coins, inventory, equipment, and skills with what it saw before.

The pipeline runs one session, then 24 at once, on every merge. A mismatch fails the session with the step, the expected value, and the actual one.

The first thing it found was that killing a monster gave no experience. The method that should have recorded who dealt how much damage notified quests and stopped there, so when the monster died the reward code found nobody to pay. For as long as nothing in the pipeline had killed anything, nothing had noticed.

The fix became a rule for who earns what (#263). Experience and SP follow the HP each player or party actually took off the monster. Items follow the same contribution, weighted by level: a player needs 10% of the total to take part, and one who did 60% takes the items. A party shares experience, SP, and coins equally. Only those alive and in range when the monster dies are paid.

The rest of what the test found was in the test. Twenty-four clients at once against one server are a small distributed system, and the client had the bugs of one (#257):

  • A timeout in the middle of a packet. When a read timed out with half a frame in hand, the client dropped those bytes, and the next read started in the middle of a packet. It now keeps them and continues the frame.
  • Two hunters, one beast. A monster wanders up to 200 units each way from its spawn. The foragers' spawns are 240 apart. Two neighbours could stand on the same spot, and two sessions would walk to the same one. Each beast is now claimed in a registry shared by the whole launch before anyone walks to it, and released when it dies or leaves view.
  • A first target that came back. The launch checks at the end that every session had a first target of its own. A released beast could become another session's first target, so the check would fail by chance, some day, on someone else's pull request. First targets are now remembered for the whole launch.

On the commit that became 3.2.0, all 24 sessions finished the hunt in 120 seconds, and the server log of the run had no warning and no error. The job fails if it has either.

What it cost

  • The earlier releases. Their images and archives carried the old world, so they are no longer published. Their pages remain as history.
  • Items of old characters. A character created on the old world keeps its database rows, but items whose templates the new world lacks are skipped when the inventory loads, with one warning. In the upgrade rehearsal, a character from the previous image lost sight of seven items. Under a world that has those templates, the same rows load again.
  • A small world. Three kinds of beast, levels 1 to 5, one shop. That is the honest size of what has been written so far.
  • The client's names. Items and skills keep the names the client knows until the server talks to a client that asks it.
  • Systems with nothing to run. The engine still carries code for parts of the old game that the island has no use for. Each one now has to cope with an absent world, and removing them is the next piece of work.

How it is verified

  • A step of the world job fails if the inherited datapack, its script list, or game data outside world/ reappears in the tree.
  • world check runs on every pull request: the rendered world must equal a fresh render, and every text must be ASCII.
  • The image job compares the world inside the image with the repository, 28 files by SHA-256.
  • The empty-world job starts the image with no world and expects it to reach readiness.
  • The seed-world job sends one client, then 24, through the hunt, and fails on any warning or error in the server log.
  • The image job still restarts the server, restores a backup into a clean stack, and upgrades the published image in place, on the new world.

Pushkin's ships only sail past Buyan. This server drops anchor there. The island is small: two people, seventy-two beasts, six things to buy. But every file on it was written for it, every word on it can be renamed in one place, and on every merge twenty-four players go out to hunt on it.
