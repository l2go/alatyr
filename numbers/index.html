The numerology of a revival, with a p-value

A site full of numbers invites numerology. We did it properly: a method, a table, and a p‑value. The best finding was an error.

This site is full of numbers: dates, counts, ports, versions. Numbers attract patterns the way a lit tower attracts moths, and some traditions read meaning into them. So we read the site the way a numerologist would, carefully and honestly, and then asked a statistician what it was worth.

The short version. Numerology predicts nothing. Every pattern below is real arithmetic on real facts, and every one of them is the kind of coincidence that a set of 28 numbers produces more often than not. The last section explains why, and what the exercise found that actually mattered.

The method

Reduction. Add the digits of a number, and repeat until one digit is left. The date 2006-11-15 becomes 2+0+0+6+1+1+1+5 = 16, then 1+6 = 7. Tradition keeps 11 and 22 whole as "master numbers".

Gematria. Classical gematria gives Hebrew letters numeric values. For English text there is a plain version, the English ordinal: A = 1, B = 2, and so on to Z = 26. Digits inside a word count as themselves.

We took 28 numbers from the pages of this site, each checked against the repository, and a handful of words that the site keeps returning to.

The skeptic's corner

Reduction maps every number to one of nine digits, or eleven results if the master numbers count on their own. With 28 numbers, each digit expects about three hits. The number of hits per digit follows, closely enough, a Poisson distribution with λ ≈ 3.1.

EventProbability
A given digit gets five or more of the 28 numbers≈ 20%
Some digit, any of the nine, gets five or more≈ 87%
The same, counting 11 and 22 as their own results≈ 74%

Five sixes, then, are what 28 numbers usually do. Then there is the freedom of choice, which is the engine of every gematria: which words to count, with or without spaces and digits, whole dates or years, master numbers kept or reduced. Each choice doubles the chances. After twenty words, 42, 108, and 89 will turn up; they are famous, so we notice them, and we do not notice 53 or 148. That is the multiple-comparisons problem and confirmation bias, working together.

The best finding was an error

The first draft of this analysis ran on the numbers the site showed at the time: 8,215 commits by 25 people, a revival that began on 26 September, and sixteen days to 3.0.0. On those numbers numerology found six sevens, "the number of a completed cycle", and read the start date as a nine, the number of the citadel. It was a convincing story.

Recounting every number for this page showed that three of them were wrong. The earlier count of commits included the commits of the revival itself; before it there were 8,044, by 24 people. The revival began on 18 September, so 3.0.0 came 24 days later, not 16. The site was corrected. On the true numbers the sevens drop to four, six takes the lead, and the start date reduces to one, "the number of beginnings". The new story is exactly as convincing as the old one.

Numerology found meaning in wrong data, and in right data, equally well. Checking the data is what found the error. That is the whole argument for invariants.

Reproduce it

def digits(n):
    return sum(int(d) for d in str(n))

def reduce(n):
    while n > 9 and n not in (11, 22, 33):
        n = digits(n)
    return n

def ordinal(word):
    letters = sum(ord(c) - 64 for c in word.upper() if c.isalpha())
    return letters + sum(int(c) for c in word if c.isdigit())

print(reduce(20260918))                         # 1
print(ordinal("INVARIANT"))                     # 108
print(ordinal("L2JFREE"), ordinal("SIGNED"))    # 58 58
print(ordinal("ALATYR"), ordinal("ROOTED"))     # 77 77
